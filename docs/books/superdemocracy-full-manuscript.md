### Chapter One: Democracy

Demokratia, the original Greek word, is demos ‘people’ and kratos ‘power,’ it seems the old rallying cry “power to the people” is really quite on the nose no matter how it’s been co-opted by various activist groups. I feel Abraham Lincoln embodied the idea beautifully when he said after the American Civil War’s deadliest battle, “that these dead shall not have died in vain. That this nation, under God, shall have a new birth of freedom. And that government of the people, for the people, and by the people shall not perish from the earth.” Wartime ooh rah aside, I think he nailed it when he said “government of the people, for the people, and by the people.”

Oxford Languages defines democracy as “a system of government by the whole population or all the eligible members of a state…”

Representative democracy or a “Republic” is what we have today, not to be confused for the true thing itself. It is when the people vote for a representative who participates in law making on their behalf. There is no direct link between the people and the law-making process outside of general elections for representatives every several years. This was a necessary adaptation in the time before internet communication.

According to Wikipedia, “Direct democracy or pure democracy is a form of democracy in which the electorate decides on policy initiatives without legislative representatives as proxies. This differs from the majority of currently established democracies, which are representative democracies (Republics). The theory and practice of direct democracy and participation as its common characteristic was the core work of many theorists, philosophers, politicians, and social critics, among whom the most important are Jean Jacques Rousseau, John Stuart Mill, and G.D.H. Cole.”

A semi-direct democracy is, as Wikipedia defines it: government “in which representatives administer day-to-day governance, but the citizens remain sovereign, allow for three forms of popular action: referendum (plebiscite), initiative, and recall. The first 2 forms, referendums and initiatives, are examples of direct legislation.” With popular action, a citizen can initiate a process at any time which can result in a new law or action by government. Switzerland is the best example of this updated model.

Liquid democracy is similar to semi-direct except it is a more fluid blend of direct and representative democracy. What qualifies it as “liquid” is the fluidity with which one can either choose a representative or vote directly on a whim. It is typically described as being able to delegate all votes on a given topic to an individual, and to just as easily take them away.

Even the question, “Which type of democracy to use?” can be up for vote, and likely would be at some point. Different regions will select for different types as best fits their needs, not what best fits their elites. There are many more types of democracy than I ever realised and I am not an expert on the different approaches. I only want to provide a vehicle for us to easily switch between them as necessary. But I will summarize and make a recommendation as to what I believe would be a good place to start.

Direct

Greek direct democracy in 5th century B.C. Athens was the first of any recorded democracy. Each year 500 names were chosen from among the population allowed to participate who would actively handle all day to day proceedings of the government. At this time only free males were allowed to participate and be referred to as “citizens.” When a new law was proposed all citizens had an opportunity to vote on it in person, if you were rich enough to leave your home and your crops unattended. At an ecclesia, the meeting where these votes happened from once a month to every four months, even the subjects of whether or not to declare war and what type of military strategy should be used were voted on and discussed. The assembly could vote to ostracize any citizen who became too influential, too powerful or dangerous. Liquid

Liquid democracy is the choice for every user between direct and representative at will, and to choose your representative from among a much larger and more specialist pool of candidates. ### Chapter Eight: Government as a Platform

Government as a platform or “GaaP” is a concept coined by Tim O’Reilly in the first chapter of the book, “Open Government: Collaboration, Transparency & Participation in Practice,” by Daniel Lathrop and Laura Ruman.

The concept is broadly, platform thinking, which is to build something which enables others to do more. Things like bridges, roads, town centers, satellites, television and of course social media platforms. Amazon has created a platform where many people can buy and sell things, it largely just facilitates the transactions. Google has created an information shortcut, and with that it facilitates anyone to learn anything. Facebook and Twitter are platforms for social interaction, where people can build groups of (almost) any kind, can use the tools put in place in so many different ways and connect with people near and far. Spotify and YouTube are places where any and all artists of a certain medium can post their work. Superdemocracy will facilitate social co-operation, it aims to be a platform, with a set of tools to facilitate peaceful collaboration, with an ease that we have never seen before.

The common thread with all of these companies (except the last because it hasn’t been built yet) is that they have built platforms that the users themselves add value to. These platforms aim to only be the vessel for the people’s use by giving them a set of tools to use and letting them go free, to play and experiment each in their own way. Each company, and governments, have made key investments in infrastructure to facilitate action. These investments have a multiplier effect of what the people, and commerce, want and need.

Mr. O’Reilly poses the questions of “gov 2.0:” “if government is a platform, how can we use technology to make it into a better platform?” “What lessons can government take from the success of competitive platforms as it tries to harness the power of technology to remake government?”
